Ritual one: the base rate question. Every time a success story is offered to you as evidence, ask one question before you draw any inference at all. Out of how many? If a coaching program shows you ten people who got rich, ask how many bought the program. If it's ten out of ten thousand, the success rate is 0.1%. The story is true. The strategy is a lottery dressed as a method. You're not asking this to be cynical. You're asking it because your brain physically cannot supply the denominator on its own - the denominator is the missing planes, and you have to drag them up by hand.

Ritual two: the graveyard interview. Once per major decision - once per career change, once per investment, once per business - go find someone who tried the same thing and failed. Not to be discouraged. To complete the dataset your brain refuses to complete. Their story will be less dramatic. They will be harder to find. They will be more useful than anyone who succeeded. If you cannot find a failure to talk to, that is itself a data point - it likely means the failures have been deliberately filtered out of your view.

Ritual three: the pre-mortem. Gary Klein wrote about this in the Harvard Business Review in September 2007 and the technique is criminally underused. Before you start a project - before any major decision - sit down and assume it has already failed. Write down, in present tense, what went wrong. Klein found, with real teams, that this exercise can roughly double the number of risks the team identifies, compared to ordinary risk planning. It works for one reason. It is the only way to force your visible-data system to retrieve evidence that doesn't exist yet. You are pre-loading the missing planes into the conversation.

Ritual four: the falsification check. When you consume advice - a book, a podcast, a mentor - ask one question after. What evidence, if it existed, would prove this advice wrong? If nothing could, you're not learning. You're being inducted into a survivor's mythology. Falsifiability is the line between knowledge and folklore, and in the age of testimonial-driven content, the line keeps moving.

You will notice these are not insights. They are chores. That is the point. Survivorship bias cannot be defeated with cleverness, because the cleverness is the thing being fooled. It can only be defeated by friction - the small, scheduled, slightly annoying habit of asking the question your brain doesn't want to ask.
