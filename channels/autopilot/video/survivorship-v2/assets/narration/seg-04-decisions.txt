And here's where it stops being abstract. Career. You study what someone successful did at twenty-four, and you try to reverse-engineer the path. What you don't know — what you literally cannot find out from your normal sources — is how many people quit stable jobs to write novels and never got published. How many left engineering to chase music and never broke through. How many took the exact same leap you're considering, with the exact same gifts, and ended up older and broker and quieter. The path you're admiring is real. The base rate is hidden. Relationships. You take dating advice from couples who lasted thirty years. You don't survey the people who followed identical advice and divorced. The advice survived. The couple survived. So you assume the advice caused the survival. Sometimes it did. Often it didn't. The actual ratio is, by design, unknowable from the inside. Investing. You remember the 2018 Bessembinder study in the Journal of Financial Economics. Just four percent of U.S. stocks created the entire net wealth gain of the stock market between 1926 and 2016. The other ninety-six percent collectively performed about as well as short-term Treasury bills. So every stock-picker telling you about their winning trade is, statistically, a survivor pulled from a small lucky sample. The thousands who blew up their portfolios using the same strategy — same chart-reading, same discipline, same Substack — don't get podcasts. They get day jobs. Risk-taking in general. You read a founder's story — quit the job, bet the savings, made it work — and you feel inspired. You're seeing the survivor. The actuarial reality, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Business Employment Dynamics data, is that around 20.4% of new businesses fail in their first year and 65.3% fail within ten. The hero story isn't fake. It's one data point pulled from a graveyard of identical-looking attempts. Now there's a fair objection here. Someone's been waiting to make it. "Sure," they'll say, "but isn't there some signal in the survivors? Wouldn't you rather take advice from someone who did it than from someone who didn't?" And the answer is — yes, with a knife edge of asterisks. There is sometimes signal. The problem is you cannot tell signal from noise from inside the sample. If the strategy is high-variance — if a hundred people tried it and one became a billionaire and ninety-nine became baristas — then the survivor's testimony is evidence the strategy is high-variance, not evidence the strategy is good. They are two different claims. The survivor cannot tell you which one their story is. Often, they don't know themselves. I am going to admit something here that you've earned, since you've stayed this long. For about three years of my own twenties, I followed an investing strategy I had picked up from a person whose results were, on paper, undeniable. The numbers were real. The reasoning was thoughtful. The track record was years long. What I didn't know — what I learned, much later, when the person stopped publishing — was that they had been one of seven students of a particular method, and the other six had gone bankrupt over the same decade. The strategy was a coin that had been flipped seven times, and I was being taught by the one heads. I was lucky. I lost a small amount of money instead of a large amount of it. Most people who get the same lesson don't catch it in time. The lesson cost me a year of savings and the certainty that 'I do my homework before I invest' was actually protecting me. So: the mistake isn't admiring the survivor. The mistake is using them as evidence for what your own decision is likely to produce. Inspiration without base rates is a coin toss that someone happened to win, repackaged and sold to you as a strategy. And now — step back from your own decisions for a moment. Because here is the part that makes all of this not just an information problem. If we can see this — if we can name it, if we can verify the numbers, if every single field of decision-making has been screaming about this for fifty years — why does nobody act on it? Why do the same people read the same kinds of books next year? Why do you, even after this video, almost certainly slide back into the same way of looking? It's not that you're undisciplined. It's that the fix is not 'more information.' And to see why, we have to go one layer deeper than industry — past the books, past the algorithm — all the way to the inside of your skull.
