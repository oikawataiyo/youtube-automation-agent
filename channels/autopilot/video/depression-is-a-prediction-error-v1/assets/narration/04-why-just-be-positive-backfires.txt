And this is where the well-meaning advice gets dangerous. The STAR*D trial, led by A. John Rush and published in 2006 in the American Journal of Psychiatry, followed thousands of patients through real-world antidepressant treatment. The first-step remission rate — patients who fully recovered on the first medication — was roughly one in three. By the fourth step, the cumulative remission rate was around sixty-seven percent, leaving a substantial group whom the standard model couldn't reach at all. These are not lazy patients. These are the ones the chemical-imbalance map fails on first contact. So what does the surrounding culture tell them. Try harder. Think differently. Be positive. Daniel Wegner, in 1994, in Psychological Review, gave us the ironic process theory — the deliberate attempt not to think a thing makes the thing more available, not less. Tell yourself not to feel hopeless and you've just rehearsed hopelessness once more, with a louder voice. Joanne Wood and colleagues, in 2009, in Psychological Science, ran a study that should have ended the affirmation industry. They asked low-self-esteem subjects to repeat the affirmation I am a lovable person several times. Mood went down. Self-esteem went down. The affirmation collided with the listener's actual prediction model and lost. So the brain treated the affirmation as evidence of how far the speaker was from the claim. In the predictive frame this is obvious. Forcing a positive prediction that the brain cannot connect to lived data doesn't close the prediction error. It widens it. The model logs the optimism as noise. The depression hardens. The well-meaning advice becomes, mechanically, anti-medicine.
