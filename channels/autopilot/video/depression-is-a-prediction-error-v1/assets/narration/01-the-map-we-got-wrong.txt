The chemical-imbalance story has a date of birth. Joseph Schildkraut, in 1965, published the catecholamine hypothesis of affective disorders in the American Journal of Psychiatry. The reasoning was honest but indirect. Drugs that boosted certain neurotransmitters seemed to lift mood. So the disorder, the thinking went, must be a deficit of those neurotransmitters. The theory was reverse-engineered from the medicine, not measured in the brain. And the medicine, in the 1980s and 90s, became a product. The serotonin variant of the story — that depression is what happens when you don't have enough serotonin — was easier to draw on a pharmaceutical ad than the truth, and it traveled further than any peer-reviewed result behind it. In 2022, Joanna Moncrieff and colleagues at University College London published a systematic umbrella review in Molecular Psychiatry. They aggregated decades of research across serotonin metabolites, receptor density, transporter studies, and depletion experiments. Their conclusion was unusually blunt for a journal of that caliber. There is no consistent evidence that depression is caused by a deficiency of serotonin. The medication may still help some people, but not for the reason the story said. The lay public had absorbed a model that was scaffolding, not biology. And the cost of that model wasn't only intellectual. If you were told your sadness was a missing nutrient, then the moment the missing nutrient didn't help, the implication landed on you. You weren't even broken in the right way. The map was wrong. The territory was bigger.
