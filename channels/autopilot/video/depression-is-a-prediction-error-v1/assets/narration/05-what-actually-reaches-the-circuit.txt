Okay. So what does work. The honest answer is that several different things do, and they work for reasons that finally line up with the predictive frame. First. Behavioral activation. The lineage runs from Peter Lewinsohn in the 1970s to Sona Dimidjian and colleagues in 2006 in the Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, where a randomized trial showed behavioral activation matching cognitive therapy and outperforming antidepressant medication for severely depressed patients. The instruction is almost boringly concrete. Schedule small, specific, low-risk actions, and let mood lag behind action. The mechanism, in predictive language, is that you give the model a series of tiny forecasts it can quickly confirm. The bed got made. The walk happened. The friend was called. Each completion is a small piece of evidence that the future is not, in fact, a vacuum that swallows effort. The model updates from the bottom. Second. Rapid-acting antidepressants that bypass the monoamine system. Robert Berman and colleagues, in 2000, in Biological Psychiatry, showed that a single subanesthetic dose of ketamine produced significant antidepressant effects within hours, not weeks. John Krystal and colleagues, in 2019, in Cell, described why this was a paradigm shift — by acting on NMDA glutamate receptors and triggering rapid synaptic plasticity, ketamine appears to reset the prediction substrate, opening a window in which the brain can learn a new forecast. Third. Sleep, light, and movement. Matthew Walker, in his 2017 book Why We Sleep, gathered the evidence that sleep loss is one of the most reliable amplifiers of depressive symptoms and one of the easiest predictors to correct. Predictive coding doesn't work on a brain that's been denied the offline period it uses to consolidate yesterday's data. And finally. Therapy, when it updates predictions rather than just rearranging thoughts. The cognitive behavioral lineage from Aaron Beck onward has always implicitly been a prediction-correction enterprise. Done well, it works. You don't argue with depression. You feed the model evidence it can no longer ignore.
