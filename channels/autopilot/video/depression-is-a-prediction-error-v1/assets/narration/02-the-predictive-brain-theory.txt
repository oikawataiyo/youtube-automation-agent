Here's the frame that's quietly replacing it. Karl Friston, in 2010, in Nature Reviews Neuroscience, articulated the free-energy principle — the idea that the brain is constantly minimizing prediction error. Everything it does, from perception to movement to mood, can be understood as the management of a forecast about the next moment. Lisa Feldman Barrett and W. Kyle Simmons, in a 2015 paper in Nature Reviews Neuroscience, took that frame and turned it inward. They argued that the brain isn't just predicting what's out there. It's predicting what's in here. Heart rate. Gut tension. Energy reserves. Inflammation. The technical word is interoception, and the consequence is enormous. Mood, in this view, is not a feeling about the world. It's the brain's forecast about whether the body's resources can meet the world's demands. Then Klaas Stephan and colleagues, in 2016, in Lancet Psychiatry, fitted depression directly into that frame. They described it as allostatic dysregulation — a state in which the brain's prediction of future effort and reward has flattened or inverted. Whatever you do, the model says, won't be worth the energy it will cost. So motivation stops. Movement stops. Effort stops. From the inside it feels like there's no point. From the model's perspective, you are obeying a forecast that has stopped believing the future will pay you back. Depression isn't low mood. It's an energy budget drawn by a brain that has stopped expecting the outside world to refund the deposit.
