And the prediction is being fed by data the head alone can't override. Charles Raison and Andrew Miller, in 2011, in Current Psychiatry Reports, gathered a body of work showing what clinicians had been seeing for decades — that systemic inflammation tracks with depressive symptoms in ways that don't fit the serotonin story at all. Patients given interferon for hepatitis treatment, which spikes inflammatory cytokines, frequently developed full-blown depressive episodes. People with chronic inflammatory conditions had depression rates several times the baseline. Something in the immune system was talking to the mood circuit, in a voice the old model wasn't equipped to hear. Jennifer Felger, in a 2019 review in Pharmacology and Therapeutics, showed the mechanism more precisely. Peripheral inflammation, signaled to the brain via cytokines and the vagus nerve, suppresses dopamine release in the basal ganglia. The exact circuit responsible for motivation and reward goes quiet, not because the chemistry is missing, but because the body's surveillance system has decided this is not the moment to spend energy on the future. The result, on a screen, looks like depression. From inside the body, it feels like depression. It is depression. Ole Köhler-Forsberg and colleagues, in a 2019 meta-analysis in Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica, showed that anti-inflammatory adjuncts produced measurable benefits in a subset of patients — particularly those whose blood markers actually showed elevated inflammation. Not everyone. Not as a slogan. But enough to confirm the chain. The mood you couldn't shake was, in some cases, an immune system in the wrong key. The medicine that argued only with serotonin was sometimes arguing with the wrong organ.
