Okay. So what do you actually do with a reflex you cannot outthink. The first move is humility. You do not shame a spinal reflex into becoming philosophical. You work from the body upward. Levine (1997, Waking the Tiger) argued that trauma recovery often involves completing thwarted defensive energy through the body rather than trying to reason it away. That does not mean every person needs the same method. It means the direction matters. Bottom-up before top-down. If freeze is a survival state, the body has to receive evidence that survival is no longer at stake. Porges (2007, Biological Psychology) would call these cues of safety: a steady voice, a nonthreatening face, orientation to the room, a slower exhale, enough distance to choose movement. Force often backfires because force resembles danger. Lehrer and Gevirtz (2014, Frontiers in Psychology) reviewed heart-rate-variability biofeedback and showed how slow breathing practices can strengthen regulation by working with the body's rhythm rather than against it. That is not a magic cure. It is threshold training. Over time, the system may need more threat before it drops into freeze, and less time before it comes back. For some people, the first sign of recovery is not confidence. It is one finger moving, one breath lengthening, one second of orientation returning before the old shutdown completes its descent. Then there is the cognitive piece, but it comes after naming the biology. When tonic immobility is understood as a reflex, the self-blame loop loses its premise. The question changes from, why was I so weak, to, what did my body believe was impossible. That question is kinder, but it is also more precise. You do not beat the freeze by being braver. You make your body believe, sooner, that the danger is gone.
