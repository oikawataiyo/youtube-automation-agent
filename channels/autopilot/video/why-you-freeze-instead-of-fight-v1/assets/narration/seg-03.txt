And this is where it gets worse. We have built a whole language of self-command around moments when command may be the least available thing in the room. Just set a boundary. Just walk away. Just say no. Sometimes those sentences are useful before danger arrives. But during the freeze itself, they can become a second accusation. LeDoux (1996, The Emotional Brain) showed that fear does not wait politely for conscious interpretation; fast subcortical routes can activate defensive responses before the cortex has assembled a story. So the question, why did I not decide differently, often assumes the wrong narrator. The part of you that writes reasons may not have been the part in control. This is not abstract. Bovin, Jager-Hyman, Gold, Marx and Sloan (2008, Journal of Traumatic Stress) found that tonic immobility during sexual assault was tied to peritraumatic fear, perceived inescapability, and later PTSD symptoms. Volchan, Souza, Franklin and colleagues (2011, Biological Psychology) reported biological evidence of tonic immobility in human victims of traumatic stress. In other words, the frozen body is not rare, theatrical, or chosen. It is common enough to study, measurable enough to document, and painful enough to predict later suffering. Yet survivors are routinely asked why they let it happen, as if a person in tonic immobility is weighing options like someone choosing a restaurant. The self-help version of the world wants every wound to become a lesson in assertiveness, because that keeps the story clean. If the problem was your skill, then the product can be a new skill. But a brainstem reflex is not impressed by slogans. A culture that sells composure as a lifestyle quietly tells the frozen that they simply did not try hard enough. And once shame attaches itself to the reflex, the body learns to fear its own defenses.
