And this is where the feed stops being neutral. It is not just showing you people. It is giving your brain a measuring stick that never touches the floor. Festinger argued that humans have a built-in drive to evaluate themselves by comparison, especially when there is no objective standard. Am I happy enough. Successful enough. Loved enough. Normal enough. The feed answers by handing you thousands of upward comparisons, each one edited down to a moment that survived its owner's insecurity. Chou and Edge found that heavier Facebook users were more likely to believe that other people were happier and had better lives, and Vogel et al. found that frequent social media comparison predicted lower self-esteem. That is the mechanism hiding inside the paradox. You came for connection, but you were placed on a treadmill of evaluation. Every scroll says, compare your ordinary Tuesday to someone's best dinner. Compare your tired face to their best angle. Compare your unmade bed to their vacation balcony. None of these posts have to be fake to distort you. The distortion comes from sample selection. People post what feels shareable, and what feels shareable is usually the version of life that survived embarrassment. So your brain, which evolved to scan the group for status and belonging, starts treating a curated global feed as if it were the local tribe. But the tribe is impossible. Too attractive. Too successful. Too socially alive. You can never catch it, because it was never one real group of people in the first place. It was a composite ghost built from everyone's brightest five seconds. And once comparison replaces connection, loneliness starts to make terrible sense.
