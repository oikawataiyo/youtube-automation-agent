This is where the distinction matters, because the problem is not simply screens. It is passive exposure pretending to be participation. Verduyn et al. found that passive Facebook use undermined affective well-being, while active interaction did not carry the same cost. The silent scroll is the corrosive part. You watch. You absorb. You compare. You provide your nervous system with social cues but no reciprocal exchange. No voice. No timing. No mutual attention. Twenge, Spitzberg, and Campbell found that as screen-based contact replaced in-person time among U.S. adolescents, loneliness rose, and Hunt, Marx, Lipson, and Young found experimentally that limiting social media to thirty minutes a day reduced loneliness and depression. Notice what that implies. The answer is not moral purity. It is not throwing the phone into the sea and pretending modern life did not happen. The answer is understanding dose and type. A message from a friend can count. A call can count. A strange little honest comment can count. But an hour of lurking through other people's rooms usually does not. It is social sugar: sweet on the tongue, thin in the blood. It gives you the flavor of belonging without the nutrients. And because it almost counts, it is more dangerous than obvious isolation. If you were simply alone, the signal would be clear. You might call someone. You might leave the apartment. But passive scrolling confuses the signal. It lets you feel as if you have done something relational while your body is still waiting for another nervous system to answer back. So the question becomes practical, not philosophical. What kind of contact actually counts.
