Here is the thing about pulling away. It usually begins long before dating has a name. In Bowlby's 1969 Attachment and Loss from Basic Books, the infant is not described as needy in some embarrassing personal way. The infant has an attachment behavioral system, a biological program that turns on under threat and moves the child toward a caregiver. Hunger, fear, pain, separation. The body says, find the person. The tragedy is that children also learn what happens when they do. In Ainsworth, Blehar, Waters, and Wall's 1978 Patterns of Attachment from Erlbaum, the Strange Situation showed avoidant infants who seemed strangely calm when the caregiver returned. They looked away. They explored. They did not reach, protest, or collapse. But the meaning was not peace. Their bodies were still distressed. The need had not disappeared. It had gone underground. That is the first blueprint. If reaching gets you ignored, shamed, or met with a blank face, then not reaching becomes intelligent. The child does not decide, I will become distant. The child learns, showing need does not pay. So the face goes quiet. The hands stay busy. The room becomes safer than the person. This is why avoidant attachment can look like independence from the outside and feel like isolation from the inside. The need is still there, but it has been trained to arrive wearing another costume. Boredom. Irritation. The sudden feeling that this person is too much. What looks like not caring may be a very old kind of caring that learned to hide before anyone could punish it. And that is how the blueprint follows you into adult love.
