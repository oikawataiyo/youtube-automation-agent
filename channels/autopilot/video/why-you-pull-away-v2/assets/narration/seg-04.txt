And this is where it starts hurting other people in a way you can honestly miss. The strategy fires hardest at the moment connection is most needed. In Simpson, Rholes, and Nelligan's 1992 study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, avoidant partners under anxiety-provoking conditions sought less support and gave less support. That is the cruel math. When stress rises, the person who loves you may reach. Your body may read that reach as demand. So you become flat, busy, analytical, or unavailable. They experience rejection. You experience pressure. Both people think they are responding to the other person, but both are responding to a forecast. Your forecast says closeness will swallow me. Their forecast says distance means I am unwanted. Gross and Levenson's 1997 study in the Journal of Abnormal Psychology found that hiding emotion can carry physiological costs and does not necessarily reduce the inner experience. It reduces the sharing of it. That distinction is everything. You may look calm because you have stopped transmitting. But inside, the system is still working. The heart still knows. The muscles still brace. The grief still has to go somewhere. And the partner is left with the worst possible data: a face with no access. Over time, this creates a relationship where one person is always knocking and the other person is always proving they do not need a door. The wall did have a purpose. It kept a child from the repeated humiliation of asking for comfort where comfort was not reliably available. But adulthood changes the equation. A wall that once prevented pain can now prevent repair. It can turn every loving person into someone standing outside, slowly learning to stop knocking. The cost is not just loneliness. It is the loss of evidence that closeness could have been different.
