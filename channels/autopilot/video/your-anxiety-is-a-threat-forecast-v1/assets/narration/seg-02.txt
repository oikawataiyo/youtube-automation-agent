And this is where it gets worse. Anxiety does not need a monster. It can live on a blank space. A 2013 paper by Grupe and Nitschke in Nature Reviews Neuroscience described uncertainty and anticipation as central engines of anxiety, where the brain overestimates the probability and cost of threat while treating unresolved information as pressure. Not danger, exactly. The possibility of danger. The open tab in the mind that refuses to close. That is why anxiety can feel strongest when nothing has happened yet. The anxious mind is not only afraid of bad outcomes. It is afraid of the interval before the outcome becomes known. Waiting for the test result. Watching the typing bubble. Replaying the conversation because silence has too much room inside it. The pain is not just that something might go wrong. The pain is that you cannot make the world answer immediately. Carleton went even further in a 2016 Journal of Anxiety Disorders paper, arguing that fear of the unknown may be a fundamental fear beneath many others. On that view, the brain would rather create a terrible answer than remain inside a question. Certainty hurts less than ambiguity, even when the certainty is invented. So anxiety becomes a grim little analyst, filling missing data with the worst plausible story because an ugly forecast still feels more manageable than no forecast at all. This is why reassurance wears out so quickly. You calm one question, and the system generates another. Reassurance answers content, but anxiety is often fighting form. It is not fear of a thing. It is the refusal to remain inside a question. And if uncertainty is the fuel, the body is where the forecast becomes convincing.
