And here is where anxiety becomes less like an alarm and more like a teacher with terrible methods. Every time you escape, check, postpone, overprepare, ask for reassurance, sit near the exit, reread the message, or cancel at the last second, you feel relief. Relief is powerful. Relief is also training data. Borkovec, Alcaine, and Behar argued in a 2004 Guilford chapter on the avoidance theory of worry that worry itself can function as cognitive avoidance, keeping frightening images vague enough that the brain never fully learns they were survivable. In other words, worry can look like preparation while acting like protection from learning. Salkovskis made a related point in a 1991 Behavioural Psychotherapy paper about safety behaviours. If you only survive the room because you kept your hand on the door, your brain never learns the room was safe. If you only survive the conversation because you rehearsed every sentence, your brain never learns you could have been imperfect and still been okay. The behaviour prevents disconfirmation. The forecast says danger. The escape says good thing we ran. The system updates in exactly the wrong direction. This is the cruelty of avoidance. It works in the short term, which is why it is so persuasive. The email remains unread, and your body softens. The party is skipped, and your breathing returns. The doctor is not called, and for one evening you do not have to know. But the next time the same situation appears, the forecast comes back louder because the last escape was recorded as survival. The anxious brain does not say, we avoided and nothing happened. It says, nothing happened because we avoided. So anxiety survives not because the prediction keeps being accurate, but because it keeps being protected from correction. You keep proving it right by running from the test. Which means the way out cannot be another argument with the forecast. It has to be a different kind of learning.
