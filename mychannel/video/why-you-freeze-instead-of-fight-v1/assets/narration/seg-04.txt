Here is the part people rarely describe cleanly. Freeze is not always a dramatic statue moment. Sometimes it is fog. Sometimes it is the room getting far away. Sometimes time slows, your voice disappears, and you watch yourself from somewhere behind your own eyes. Schauer and Elbert (2010, Zeitschrift fur Psychologie / Journal of Psychology) described dissociation after traumatic stress along a freeze-to-faint continuum, where immobility, depersonalization, analgesia, and collapse can emerge as later defenses in the same cascade. Kozlowska, Walker, McLean and Carrive (2015, Harvard Review of Psychiatry) also placed tonic immobility and collapsed states below fight-or-flight in the defense cascade, which matters because these later states are often remembered as blankness rather than action. That is unsettling, but it is also relieving. It means the blankness had a function. The body narrowed sensation, slowed the scene, or separated awareness because full contact with the moment may have been too much to metabolize. Roelofs (2017, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B) described freezing as an organized defensive state, not simply the absence of behavior. A person can leave the room, the relationship, the assault, the childhood house, and still carry a nervous system braced for an event that is no longer happening. That is why you may freeze during ordinary conflict years later. A partner changes tone. A manager asks a question. Someone blocks the doorway. Your adult life says, this is manageable. Your body says, this has happened before. And because the freeze response is quiet, it can be mistaken for personality. I am avoidant. I am weak. I am bad under pressure. Maybe. But maybe the flat, foggy, cannot-move feeling is not who you are. Maybe it is an unfinished survival reflex still running in the background, waiting for proof that the danger is finally over. Which means the way out cannot just be an argument with yourself.
