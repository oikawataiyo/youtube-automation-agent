And this is where the story becomes uncomfortable. The attachment system does not retire when you get a job, a body count, and a private apartment. In Hazan and Shaver's 1987 study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, romantic love was framed as an attachment process, not just chemistry or personal preference. Adults seek proximity, safety, and a secure base through partners in ways that echo early bonds. Then Bartholomew and Horowitz's 1991 study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology gave the pattern more shape. Some people carry a positive model of themselves and a negative model of others. That is dismissing avoidance: I am fine, people are unreliable, needing is weakness. Others carry a negative model of themselves and a negative model of others. That is fearful avoidance: I want closeness, but closeness is where I expect to get hurt. Both can pull away. But they pull away with different weather inside. The dismissing person may feel numb, superior, suddenly allergic to dependence. The fearful person may feel flooded, suspicious, and ashamed for wanting the bond at all. From the outside, both may send the same text: I just need space. Inside, one is shutting the door, and the other is pressing against it while terrified of what opens. This matters because avoidant attachment is not a personality brand. It is a regulation strategy. It protected a four-year-old who had too few options. But when it runs in a thirty-year-old's relationship, it starts mistaking intimacy for danger and distance for truth. And because these models feel like common sense from the inside, the pattern rarely announces itself as fear. It arrives as a mature-sounding conclusion: this is not enough, this is moving too fast, I work better alone. The bedroom becomes the crib again, only now everyone involved thinks they are making adult choices.
