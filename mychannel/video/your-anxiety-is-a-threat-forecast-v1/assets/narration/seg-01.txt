Here is the thing about anxiety. It feels like a reaction to the present, but much of it is your nervous system voting on the future before your conscious mind has seen the ballot. In his 1996 Simon & Schuster book The Emotional Brain, Joseph LeDoux described a fast threat pathway, often called the low road, where sensory information can reach the amygdala quickly enough to prepare the body before slower cortical interpretation catches up. That is why you can tense before you know what you heard. It is not wisdom. It is speed. And speed has a cost. LeDoux and Pine argued in a 2016 American Journal of Psychiatry paper that defensive survival circuits and the conscious feeling of fear or anxiety are not the same system. One machine mobilizes the body. Another machine explains the mobilization after it has begun. So when you say, I am anxious because something bad is about to happen, you may be hearing the narrator arrive late to a scene the body already started filming. The tight chest, the shallow breath, the narrowed attention, the urge to check, leave, apologize, scan, or prepare, these can be consequences of an alarm that fired before the story knew its own plot. This matters because we usually treat anxiety as if it were a message. The email may be wrong. The relationship may be doomed. You may collapse in public. Sometimes anxiety points at something real. But very often, by the time you feel it, the forecast has already moved your body into the predicted weather. And once the body is there, the mind goes looking for a reason. So the first shift is small but brutal. Anxiety is not always evidence. Sometimes it is the smoke from a prediction that caught fire before the room was checked. And the question becomes why your brain would rather sound the alarm than sit inside not knowing.
