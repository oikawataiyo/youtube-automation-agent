Okay. So what do you actually do. You do not delete the alarm. You teach it a better estimate. Craske, Treanor, Conway, Zbozinek, and Vervliet argued in a 2014 Behaviour Research and Therapy paper that exposure works best through inhibitory learning, not by erasing fear, but by building a competing prediction. The old forecast says, if I enter this situation, I will not survive it. The new learning says, I can feel this and remain here anyway. The word anyway is doing a lot of work. Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson, in their 1999 Guilford Press book Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, built a whole clinical approach around a similar move. Stop making inner discomfort the condition that decides your life. Notice the forecast. Name it. Make room for it. Then act in the direction of what matters while it is still making noise. Not because the noise is fake. Because the noise is not the ruler. There are three practical ways this starts. First, violate expectancies gently and on purpose. If anxiety says you must check the message six times, check once and let the uncertainty burn. Second, practice small doses of not knowing. Leave one harmless question unanswered. Let one bodily sensation exist without converting it into a diagnosis. Third, relabel the body. The racing heart is a prediction, not a verdict. The heat in your face is a signal, not a sentence. The trembling is preparation energy, not proof that you are failing. None of this feels clean at first. The point is not to win an argument with anxiety in your head. The point is to give the brain new outcomes to remember. I stayed, and the feared thing did not happen. I shook, and I still spoke. I did not know, and I survived the gap. Over time, the forecast changes because the evidence changes. You do not become a person with no alarm. You become a person whose alarm has learned a better estimate.
