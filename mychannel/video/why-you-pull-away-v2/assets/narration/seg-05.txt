Okay. So what do you actually do when the door inside you starts closing? First, you stop treating the urge to flee as prophecy. Roisman, Padron, Sroufe, and Egeland's 2002 study in Child Development found that people with difficult early attachment histories could develop earned-secure attachment status. The past is a starting point, not a sentence. Mikulincer and Shaver's 2007 Attachment in Adulthood from Guilford Press argues that repeated experiences of felt security with responsive others can revise internal models over time. Not in one cinematic breakthrough. Slowly. Through enough moments where need appears and the world does not punish it. So the practice begins in the tiny pause between sensation and story. My chest is tight. I want to disappear. I am looking for flaws. This is deactivation, not necessarily truth. That sentence matters because it gives you a second option. You can ask for space without making the other person the enemy. You can say, something in me is shutting down, and I need twenty minutes, but I am coming back. You can repair the withdrawal instead of hiding inside it. Johnson's 2008 book Hold Me Tight from Little, Brown makes this central to Emotionally Focused Therapy: the couple learns to talk about the cycle itself, so the withdrawal is no longer treated as the whole truth of the person. This is not about deleting independence. It is about making room for dependence that does not feel like surrender. You do not overwrite an old blueprint by yelling at it. You overwrite it by giving the nervous system a new record, then another, then another. Someone reaches. You stay a little longer. You leave the door open. You come back.
