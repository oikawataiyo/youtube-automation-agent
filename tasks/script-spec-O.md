## TOPIC
The dominant model of self-control — that willpower is a finite fuel that drains with use, like a muscle that tires — was one of the most cited ideas in psychology, and it largely failed to replicate. "Ego depletion" looked huge in the lab until preregistered multi-lab studies and bias-corrected meta-analyses shrank the effect toward zero. The reframe matters: if willpower is not a tank you run dry, then the people who look disciplined are not out-gritting you. They are arranging their lives so the gritting is rarely needed.

## SECTION OUTLINES (5 sections, ~320 words each)

### Section 1 — "The Idea That Ate Psychology"
- Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) — the original ego-depletion experiments: resisting cookies left people quitting puzzles sooner. Self-control seemed to draw on one limited reservoir.
- Muraven & Baumeister (2000, *Psychological Bulletin*) — the "strength model": self-control resembles a muscle that fatigues with use and can be exhausted in the short term. The metaphor became gospel — and an early supportive meta-analysis, Hagger, Wood, Stiff & Chatzisarantis (2010, *Psychological Bulletin*), reported a large effect across hundreds of studies.
- Close on: for a decade, this was settled science. It was also about to break.

### Section 2 — "When the Tank Did Not Empty"
- Carter & McCullough (2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*) and Carter, Kofler, Forster & McCullough (2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*) — once you correct for publication bias and small-study effects, the evidence for a limited self-control resource nearly vanishes.
- Hagger et al. (2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) — a preregistered Registered Replication Report across 23 labs found an effect indistinguishable from zero. The flagship finding did not survive contact with its own method.
- Close on: the muscle did not get tired in the only experiments designed in advance not to fool themselves.

### Section 3 — "It Was Belief, Not Fuel"
- Job, Dweck & Walton (2010, *Psychological Science*) — depletion appears mainly in people who *believe* willpower is limited. Those who see it as abundant show no fade. The "tank" behaves like an expectation, not a substance.
- Inzlicht & Schmeichel (2012, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) — the process model: what looks like depletion is a shift in motivation and attention — away from "have-to" labor, toward what feels rewarding — not the burning of a literal resource.
- Close on: you did not run out of willpower at 9pm. Your brain re-priced the effort and stopped wanting to pay.

### Section 4 — "Effort Has a Price Tag, Not a Fuel Gauge"
- Kurzban, Duckworth, Kable & Myers (2013, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) — the opportunity-cost model: effort feels costly because your brain is constantly weighing whether this task is worth more than what else you could be doing. Fatigue is a signal, not an empty tank.
- Inzlicht, Shenhav & Olivola (2018, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) — the effort paradox: effort is experienced as costly *and* can add value and meaning. The cost is computed, contextual, and reversible — which is exactly what a depleting fuel could never be.
- Close on: "I'm out of willpower" is your brain's way of saying "this stopped being worth it" — and that is a far more hackable sentence.

### Section 5 — "What Disciplined People Actually Do"
- Stop relying on the moment of temptation — Duckworth, Gendler & Gross (2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) — situational self-control: change the environment *before* desire spikes, so willpower is never the thing standing between you and the cookie.
- Build habits, not resolve — Galla & Duckworth (2015, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) — people high in self-control succeed because good behaviors run on automatic habit, not heroic restraint. Wood & Neal (2007, *Psychological Review*) — habits are cue-driven, willpower-cheap.
- Engineer fewer temptations — Milyavskaya & Inzlicht (2017, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*) — people who reach goals report *fewer* temptations, not more successful resistance. Winning looks like never entering the fight.
- Close on: the goal was never a bigger willpower muscle. It was a life arranged so the muscle rarely has to lift.

## OUTRO BEATS
- Ego depletion is a cautionary tale about how a tidy metaphor can outrun its evidence for twenty years.
- Drop the tank, and self-discipline stops being a moral test you keep failing and becomes a design problem you can actually solve.
